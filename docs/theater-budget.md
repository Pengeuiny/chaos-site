# Budgeting and Financial Planning Framework for a Booster-Run Theater Program

## TL;DR
- **Build the season budget from the bottom up in this order: cost each show first → add shared overhead (G&A) → set ticket prices from break-even math → project earned + contributed revenue → close the gap with fundraising → get board approval → track actuals monthly → reconcile after each show.** This mirrors your instinct and is exactly what nonprofit finance experts recommend.
- **The "keep spending under control at the last minute" warning is real and preventable.** Late-stage cost creep in theater comes mostly from tech week (crew overtime, last-minute set/prop/costume buys). Control it with a per-show budget owner, a purchase-approval threshold (two signatures at $250+), a locked 10–15% contingency line, and a spending freeze once you hit tech week.
- **For a $35K–$50K season, keep no single revenue source above ~25–30% of the total, hold overhead near 20–35%, keep a contingency in each show and a 3-month operating reserve, and fund the remote chorus trip through a fair-share participation fee plus group (not individual) fundraising** — never individual fundraising accounts, which can cost you your 501(c)(3) status.

## Key Findings

1. **Your mental model is correct, and it matches best practice.** Nonprofit finance guides (Propel Nonprofits, National Council of Nonprofits) all say to build program (per-show) budgets first, then roll them up, then layer in overhead and revenue. You already have the right sequence.
2. **Per-show budgets should use a standard line-item list.** Rights/royalties, scripts/scores, sets, costumes, props, lighting, sound, makeup, programs, marketing, tech/stipends, and a contingency. For small school/community productions, royalties and sets are usually the two biggest lines.
3. **Overhead ("G&A") is normal and healthy — don't starve it.** A commonly cited healthy range for nonprofits is roughly 20–35% overhead (program spending of 65–80%). Allocate shared costs across shows by a simple, consistent rule.
4. **Ticket pricing should start from break-even, not gut feel.** Figure out fixed costs per show, divide by (price − variable cost per attendee) to get how many seats you must sell, then sanity-check against your seating capacity and what your community will pay.
5. **Diversify revenue.** No single source should dominate. Earned (tickets, concessions, ads, merch) plus contributed (donations, sponsorships, grants, fundraisers). Concessions can run very high margins; program ads are a reliable earned-income line.
6. **The remote chorus bus trip needs its own sub-budget.** Charter buses are the main driver. Fund the trip with a fair-share participation fee plus group fundraising — and stay away from individual fundraising accounts.
7. **Spending controls prevent the last-minute blowout.** Budget owners, approval thresholds, commitment tracking, a contingency line, a tech-week freeze, and post-show reconciliation.
8. **Booster governance basics matter.** Board-approved budget, a treasurer who reports monthly, dual signatures, receipts for reimbursements, separated duties, and an operating reserve.

## Details

### 1. The season budgeting workflow (the framework)

Think of it as a loop you run once a year to plan, then keep running all season to stay on track.

**Step 1 — Cost each show from the ground up (bottom-up program budgets).**
Build a separate budget for each of your 6 productions (3 revues + 3 mains) and the chorus program, using the line items in Section 2. Nonprofit budgeting guidance is explicit that you should "begin with program budgets first, not overall revenue targets" and "build each program's costs from the ground up, then consolidate them into your entity-wide budget." Your revues will be cheaper (smaller scale, simpler tech); your main shows carry the big royalty and set costs.

**Step 2 — Add shared overhead (G&A) and allocate it.**
Total your organization-wide costs that aren't tied to one show (insurance, storage, software, bank/payment fees, general marketing, filing fees) and spread them across the shows and programs using a simple rule (Section 3).

**Step 3 — Set ticket prices from break-even.**
Now that you know each show's full cost (direct + its share of overhead), calculate the break-even ticket price and the number of seats you must sell, then set tiered prices (Section 4).

**Step 4 — Project revenue, both earned and contributed.**
Estimate tickets, concessions, program-ad sales, and merch (earned), plus donations, sponsorships, grants, and fundraiser proceeds (contributed). Be conservative — nonprofit finance advisors repeatedly warn against "aspirational wish lists." Use realistic attendance based on past years.

**Step 5 — Find the gap and fill it with fundraising.**
Subtract projected revenue from total costs (shows + overhead + trip + reserve contribution). Whatever's left is your fundraising target. Plan specific fundraisers to close it — don't leave it to hope.

**Step 6 — Board approval.**
The full board reviews and adopts the budget before the season starts. An approved annual budget is treated as a basic fiduciary duty and is expected by watchdogs and grantmakers. Start this 2–3 months before your fiscal year so there's time to approve it.

**Step 7 — Track actuals monthly (budget-vs-actual).**
The budget is "a living document," not a file-and-forget. Compare actuals to budget at every board meeting. A common trigger: investigate any line that's off by more than 10%.

**Step 8 — Reconcile after each show and at year-end.**
Close out each production's books right after it ends (Section 7), capture lessons, and feed them into next season's estimates.

### 2. Building a per-show (production) budget

Standard line-item categories for a theatrical production, with realistic small-scale figures. The single best small-scale reference is a sample production budget published by Dramatics Magazine (the magazine of the Educational Theatre Association), which shows a self-written free-venue show totaling about $1,425 and lists the add-ons for a licensed title. Figures below combine that template with real reported school numbers; note these are roughly 2018-era and real 2026 costs are somewhat higher due to inflation.

| Line item | Small-scale figure / rule of thumb |
|---|---|
| **Rights/royalties** | Plays: about $60–$100 per performance for amateur/school productions; some publishers take 8–12% of box office. Popular musicals: up to about $5,000 total in royalties for a heavily-produced title; under $2,000 for a less-common one. This is usually one of your two biggest costs. |
| **Scripts (plays, purchased)** | About $10 per script/book. |
| **Scores/books (musicals, rented)** | Rental adds roughly $800 for a licensed musical; musicals also carry a refundable security deposit. |
| **Set / scenery** | ~$350 base; add $100–$300 for large set needs; paint ~$50 more. Materials = lumber, hardware, paint. |
| **Costumes** | ~$100 base per show; add ~$300 for a period show; wigs ~$25 (+$100 period). |
| **Props** | ~$75; add ~$50 for prop-heavy shows. |
| **Lighting** | ~$200 (gels, expendables); add ~$200 if renting. |
| **Sound** | $0 if you own mics; +$60 for batteries; +$100 if renting. |
| **Makeup** | ~$25; add ~$75 for fantasy/effects shows. |
| **Programs / playbills** | ~$50 printing for a small run (more below on using these to make money). |
| **Marketing** | ~$115 total for a small show (posters ~$25, printing ~$80, social ads ~$10). |
| **Tech / stipends** | Guest director/choreographer/music-director stipends vary widely; real school director stipends run about $2,000–$3,500 for the year. Pit musicians, if used, are a separate line. |
| **Contingency ("slush")** | ~$200 in the sample; better expressed as 10–15% of the show total (see Section 7). |

**How to use it:** List every line, estimate conservatively, and keep notes explaining each number so next year's team understands your math. A useful rule-of-thumb split for a whole production is roughly personnel 40% / technical 30% / marketing 15% / venue 10% / admin 5% — but at the school scale your biggest two lines will typically be royalties and sets. Scale your revues down (simpler sets, fewer costumes, lower or no royalties if self-produced/showcase) and your main shows up.

### 3. Overhead / G&A and how to allocate it

**What counts as overhead here:** insurance (general liability), storage/unit rent for sets and costumes, software (accounting, ticketing), bank and payment-processing fees, filing fees (IRS 990, state), and general marketing (website, season brochure) that isn't tied to one show.

**How much is healthy:** There is no magic number, and the "low overhead = good" idea has been formally rejected by the major watchdogs (Charity Navigator, BBB Wise Giving Alliance, Candid/GuideStar). Common benchmarks: keep program spending at roughly 65–80% of total and overhead (admin + fundraising) at roughly 20–35%. This isn't just theory for arts groups: a 2022 study by Altamimi and Liu in *Nonprofit and Voluntary Sector Quarterly*, analyzing 22,328 US arts and cultural nonprofits from 2008–2018, found that "when arts nonprofits devoted 35% of their budget to overhead, they fared best in terms of attendance" — groups that spent too little saw attendance decline about 9%, and those that spent too much fell 30%. In other words, don't chase an artificially low overhead number; that's the "starvation cycle."

**How to allocate it across shows/programs (pick one simple, consistent rule and document it):**
- **Equal split:** divide total overhead by the number of productions. Simplest; fine for a small booster club.
- **By direct cost (percent-of-direct-cost):** bigger shows absorb more overhead in proportion to their direct budget. Fairer when your main shows are much larger than revues.
- **By participants or by budget size:** e.g., allocate the chorus program its share based on head count.

The key rules from nonprofit accounting guidance: be **reasonable**, **consistent** period-to-period, and **written down** in a short cost-allocation note. For a club your size, percent-of-direct-cost is usually the best balance of fairness and simplicity.

### 4. Ticket pricing

**Start with break-even.** The formula:

> Break-even seats = Fixed costs ÷ (Ticket price − Variable cost per attendee)

- **Fixed costs** = everything that doesn't change with attendance (royalties, set, costumes, lighting, that show's overhead share).
- **Variable costs** = the few things that rise per attendee (the printed program, any per-ticket platform fee).

Run this first. If you'd have to sell more seats than your house holds just to break even, your price is too low or your costs are too high. Then:

- **Sanity-check against capacity and your community.** Community-theater audiences push back on prices that feel "Broadway-like" for volunteer casts. Reported community/school ranges cluster well below professional houses.
- **Use tiered pricing:** adult / student / senior / child; and consider premium (opening night, reserved) vs. cheaper (weekday matinee) seats.
- **Comps:** budget a small number of free/comp tickets (cast families, sponsors, reviewers) and count them — they're a real cost.
- **Fees:** if you sell online, factor the platform/processing fee (commonly ~2.9% + $0.30 per transaction) into the price or pass it through transparently.
- **Accessibility:** a "pay-what-you-can" or suggested-price night, or a low student price, keeps you accessible while your regular tiers cover costs.

### 5. Revenue / income planning

**Diversify — no single source should dominate.** The widely cited rule of thumb: no single revenue stream should be more than ~25–30% of your budget, which implies at least four healthy streams. (One caveat: some analysts note that for *growth*, focus/concentration can matter — but for a small booster club, resilience from diversification is the right priority so one bad night or lost sponsor doesn't sink the season.)

**Earned revenue:**
- **Tickets** — your anchor; priced from break-even (Section 4).
- **Concessions** — high margin. Industry sources cite concession margins that can reach 70–80% on items like popcorn and bottled water; buy wholesale and aim for at least a 100% markup over cost. Net margins after all costs are lower, but concessions are reliably profitable and easy to run.
- **Program/playbill ads** — a strong, repeatable earned line. Real school/community rate cards: roughly $150–$300 for a full page, $80–$175 half page, $40 quarter page, ~$25 eighth page; back cover commands a premium. Also sell "break-a-leg"/shout-out ads to cast families. Price ads so the program more than covers its own printing.
- **Merchandise** — show t-shirts, flowers, cast photos, branded cups (branded drinkware lets you charge more).

**Contributed revenue:**
- **Individual donations** — the most flexible (unrestricted) money; cultivate a simple annual appeal.
- **Sponsorships** — tiered business sponsorships (logo on banners, program, website); pairs naturally with ad sales.
- **Grants** — e.g., the Educational Theatre Foundation (EdTA's philanthropic arm) subsidizes new/emerging/underserved school theatre programs; local arts councils are another source.
- **Fundraisers** — events, product sales, etc., sized to close the gap you found in Step 5.

### 6. The remote chorus trip / touring sub-budget

Build this as its own mini-budget, separate from the shows.

**Cost drivers — charter buses are the big one.** 2025–2026 charter bus pricing references:
- Full-day charter rates commonly run about **$1,300–$2,200 per day** for a full-size coach, per LA Coach USA's 2026 pricing guide ("On average, charter buses cost between $1,300 and $2,200 per day, while minibuses range from $1,000 to $1,400 per day"); hourly rates run about **$150–$200/hr** with a 4–5 hour minimum.
- For a trip 2–3 hours each way, you're likely in a full-day rate per bus, possibly more if the schedule is long.
- **Add-ons that surprise people:** tolls, bus parking/layover fees, driver gratuity (commonly 5–10% of the rental in cash), and — if it becomes an overnight — driver lodging (you must provide room and board when a trip runs more than one day; drivers are legally limited to driving windows and need 8 hours of rest per 10 hours driven, which can force an overnight for a long round trip). Book early and avoid peak season (spring) for better rates.

**Other trip line items:** meals (or a meal stipend), any lodging, admission/participation fees at the destination, substitute chaperone costs, and a **10–15% trip contingency** (student-travel planners explicitly recommend a 10–15% buffer).

**How boosters fund it (and the compliance rule that matters most):**
- **Fair-share participation fee:** a per-student trip fee is standard and legitimate.
- **Group fundraising:** run trip-specific fundraisers where proceeds go to the *whole* trip/program.
- **⚠️ Do NOT use Individual Fundraising Accounts (IFAs) / "cooperative fundraising."** Crediting money a family raises against *that* family's trip cost is a private-benefit problem. Former IRS Exempt Organizations Director Lois Lerner stated that "any booster club that raises money to benefit an individual rather than the group is in violation of federal law and stands to lose its tax-exempt status," and the Tax Court upheld this reasoning in *Capital Gymnastics Booster Club v. Commissioner* (2013). Parent Booster USA warns that "if cooperative fundraising comprises any significant part of your group's total activities (think more than 5% of total funds raised are credited to the individual accounts of parents/students... although the IRS does not have a specific percentage test), then your group likely would not be considered to qualify for 501(c)(3) status," and recommends avoiding IFAs entirely. Keep all funds in the general pool and offer need-based assistance from that pool instead, so no student is priced out.

### 7. Spending controls — stopping the last-minute blowout

**Why late-stage cost creep happens in theater.** The veteran's warning is well-founded. As a show approaches opening, tech week concentrates spending: last-minute set/prop/costume purchases when the director sees the whole thing on stage, rush-buying consumables (batteries, gels, makeup), and — where you pay techs or crew — overtime as load-in and rehearsals run late. Event-production sources describe exactly this: delays turn an 8-hour crew day into 12 hours at premium pay, and "last-minute staffing is another budget killer." Everything feels urgent and "we've come too far to cut corners," so discipline breaks right at the end.

**How to prevent it:**
- **Designate a budget owner per show** (often the director or a producer) who is accountable for that show's number.
- **Purchase-approval thresholds.** Small purchases can be made by the budget owner; anything over a set amount needs a second sign-off. Booster best practice: **two signatures on any check of $250 or more**, and no pre-signed blank checks. A common tiered approach: routine buys by the budget owner, mid-size by treasurer, large by the board.
- **Track commitments, not just cash spent (encumbrance thinking).** When someone *commits* to buy something, log it against the budget immediately — don't wait for the receipt — so you always know the true remaining balance.
- **A locked contingency line.** Standard practice is a **10–15% contingency** per show (Broadway builds in a 10–15% reserve; small-show templates include a "slush" line). Treat it as a reserve that requires the budget owner's approval to tap — not a slush fund that's spent by default.
- **A tech-week spending freeze/tightening.** Since this is where creep happens, require *all* tech-week and later purchases (beyond pre-approved consumables) to get explicit sign-off. Pre-buy known consumables early, and borrow/rent set pieces and props rather than buying (keep a return log).
- **Change-order discipline.** If the director wants something not in the budget, it's a trade-off, not an add-on: cut somewhere else or draw from contingency, with the budget owner's approval.
- **Post-show reconciliation.** Within a week or two of closing, close the books: match every expense to a receipt, compare actual vs. budget line by line, note variances and why, and record what to change next time.

### 8. Nonprofit financial governance basics for a booster club

- **Board approves the budget** before the season and reviews budget-vs-actual at each meeting.
- **Treasurer** keeps the books, reconciles the bank statement monthly, and presents a treasurer's report at every meeting. Someone *without* check-signing authority should review the monthly bank statement, and a two-person committee (or outside accountant) should do an annual review/audit.
- **Separate duties.** The person who handles income shouldn't be the only one handling expenses. Count cash with two people and a signed verification form.
- **Dual signatures** on checks at/above your threshold ($250 is common); signers shouldn't be related or share a household.
- **Receipts and a written reimbursement policy.** No receipt, no reimbursement; every payment ties to an invoice or receipt; get approval before spending.
- **Dedicated bank account** in the club's legal name with its own EIN — never a personal or the school's account. Limit online-collection tools (PayPal, etc.) to collecting funds, not paying expenses.
- **Restricted vs. unrestricted funds.** Money a donor earmarks (e.g., "for the chorus trip") is *restricted* and can only be used for that purpose; track it separately. Keep enough *unrestricted* money for flexibility — advisors often suggest a healthy share of your funds be unrestricted so you can cover overhead and surprises.
- **Operating reserve.** The common benchmark is **3–6 months of operating expenses**. The Nonprofit Operating Reserves Initiative (NORI) Workgroup recommends "a minimum reserve of 25% or three months of the annual operating expense budget," with "many organizations striv[ing] to increase that reserve to six months once the three-month reserve is achieved." At the low end, hold at least enough to cover one full cycle of obligations. Build it by budgeting a small annual surplus or designating year-end surplus to the reserve; adopt a written policy for when it can be tapped.
- **Records retention.** Keep financial documents about seven years.
- **Insurance.** Carry general liability insurance to protect the club.

### 9. Templates, tools, and the master budget structure

**Tools commonly recommended for booster/theater budgeting:**
- **Booster-specific accounting software:** MoneyMinder and BoosterHub are purpose-built; many clubs also use QuickBooks or Aplos. These produce treasurer's reports, budget-vs-actual, and fund balances by program with a couple of clicks — very helpful for showing the board clean numbers.
- **Spreadsheet templates:** the Dramatics Magazine sample production budget (per-show); Nonprofit Financial Commons' "True Program Costs" allocation template (for spreading overhead); and any theater production budget template with the Section 2 line items.

**How to structure the master season budget (one view that rolls everything up):**
Build it as tabs/sections that total into one summary:
1. **One tab per production** (Revue 1–3, Main 1–3) using the Section 2 line items, each with its own contingency.
2. **One tab for the chorus trip** (Section 6).
3. **One G&A/overhead tab**, with your allocation formula pushing overhead onto each show/program.
4. **One revenue tab** listing every earned and contributed stream, with a check that no stream exceeds ~25–30% of the total.
5. **One reserve line** (your annual contribution toward the 3-month reserve).
6. **A summary/roll-up tab** that pulls the totals: total expenses (shows + trip + overhead + reserve) vs. total revenue, showing the surplus/(gap). The gap is your fundraising target.

Add a **budget-vs-actual column** next to every line so the same workbook you plan with becomes the one you track with all season.

## Recommendations

**Before the season (planning):**
1. **Build six per-show budgets and one trip budget first**, using the Section 2 line items. Put a **10–15% contingency** in each. Estimate conservatively.
2. **Total your overhead and pick one allocation rule** (percent-of-direct-cost recommended). Write it down in one sentence.
3. **Run break-even on every show** before setting prices. Set tiered prices (adult/student/senior), and decide your comp policy and whether to pass through online fees.
4. **Map revenue streams and stress-test diversification:** is any single source over ~25–30%? If tickets or one fundraiser dominate, add another stream (ads, sponsorships).
5. **Compute the fundraising gap** and assign specific fundraisers to close it.
6. **Adopt spending controls in writing:** a budget owner per show, a $250 dual-signature threshold, a tech-week sign-off rule, and a contingency-approval rule. This is your direct answer to the veteran's warning.
7. **Get the board to approve the whole thing** 2–3 months before the season.

**During the season (control):**
8. **Track budget-vs-actual monthly**, and log commitments as soon as they're made, not when receipts arrive.
9. **Enforce the tech-week tightening**: pre-buy consumables, borrow/rent instead of buy, and require sign-off on any late add.

**After each show and year-end (reconcile):**
10. **Close each show's books within two weeks**, compare to budget, and write down variances and fixes.
11. **Fund the reserve** with any surplus toward a 3-month target, and set a written reserve policy.

**Compliance guardrails (do these regardless):**
- **Never run individual fundraising accounts** for the trip or anything else — pool all funds and offer need-based aid from the pool.
- **Keep dual signatures, receipts, monthly bank-statement review by a non-signer, and an annual review.**

**Thresholds that would change the plan:**
- If **any revenue stream exceeds ~30%** of the budget, prioritize diversifying it next cycle.
- If **overhead drifts above ~35%** of total spending, review shared costs; if it's far below ~15%, check you're not starving necessary infrastructure (insurance, software).
- If **a show's actuals cross ~85–90% of its budget before opening**, freeze non-essential spending immediately — that's the early-warning line for a tech-week overrun.
- If the **reserve falls below ~3 months** of expenses, direct year-end surplus to rebuild it before adding programming.

## Caveats
- **Dollar figures are ranges, not quotes.** Royalties in particular are title-specific — licensors (MTI, Concord/Samuel French, Dramatic Publishing) quote musicals based on your seating capacity and ticket price, so get an actual quote for each title. The small-scale line-item numbers come largely from a ~2018 Educational Theatre Association sample and real school reporting; real 2026 costs (lumber, printing, fuel) are somewhat higher due to inflation.
- **Charter bus prices vary widely by region, season, and vehicle**, and most published figures come from charter-bus vendors (who have an interest in quoting attractively). Get 2–3 written quotes for your specific route and dates; confirm what's included (tolls, parking, gratuity, driver lodging).
- **Overhead "healthy ranges" are guidelines, not rules.** The sector consensus is that a rigid overhead percentage is a poor measure of health; use the ranges as a sanity check, not a target.
- **Governance specifics (dollar thresholds, signer rules, retention) vary by state and by your own bylaws.** The $250 dual-signature figure and 7-year retention are common defaults; confirm against your bylaws and state nonprofit rules.
- **Concession margins cited (70–80%) are gross markups on star items**, not net profit after all costs, staffing, and spoilage — real net is lower but still solidly positive.
- **This is general financial-management guidance, not legal, tax, or accounting advice.** For 501(c)(3) status questions (especially around trip fundraising and private benefit), consult Parent Booster USA resources or a nonprofit attorney/CPA.
